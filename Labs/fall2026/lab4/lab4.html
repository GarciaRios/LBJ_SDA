Lab 4: From Respondents to Poll Estimates

A practical introduction to survey weights

Purpose

In Lab 3, we treated each respondent as if every person contributed equally to the estimate.

Real surveys often use weights. A weight changes how much each respondent contributes to the final poll estimate.

This lab is intentionally practical. We will use R commands you already know and add only one important new function:

weighted.mean()

The main question is:

How does weighting change what we report from a poll?

There is no need to memorize a new set of survey commands. Focus on the workflow and the interpretation.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lab, you should be able to:

  • explain in plain language what a survey weight does;
  • identify the correct weight for the statewide and Latino samples;
  • calculate an unweighted estimate;
  • calculate a weighted estimate;
  • compare weighted and unweighted results;
  • inspect whether some respondents receive more or less weight;
  • produce simple weighted subgroup estimates; and
  • explain what weighting can and cannot fix.

Part II: Build a simple weight ourselves

Before using the weights supplied with the Texas poll, we will build a very simple weight from scratch.

The goal is to understand the logic.

3. Imagine a sample that does not match the population

Suppose we have a small sample with:

  • 40% men
  • 60% women

But suppose the population we want to describe is:

  • 50% men
  • 50% women

The sample does not match the population.

Men are underrepresented.

Women are overrepresented.

A simple post-stratification weight can be written as:

\[ \text{weight} = \frac{\text{population proportion}} {\text{sample proportion}} \]

For men:

\[ \frac{0.50}{0.40} = 1.25 \]

For women:

\[ \frac{0.50}{0.60} \approx 0.83 \]

So:

  • each man receives a weight of about 1.25;
  • each woman receives a weight of about 0.83.

The logic is intuitive:

  • an underrepresented group receives a weight greater than 1;
  • an overrepresented group receives a weight less than 1.

4. Create the toy dataset

toy <- tibble(
  gender = c(
    "Man", "Man", "Man", "Man",
    "Woman", "Woman", "Woman",
    "Woman", "Woman", "Woman"
  ),
  support = c(
    1, 1, 0, 0,
    1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0
  )
)

Look at the data:

toy

Count the respondents:

toy |>
  count(gender)

We have:

  • 4 men = 40% of the sample;
  • 6 women = 60% of the sample.

5. Calculate the weights

The population target is 50% men and 50% women.

For men:

0.50 / 0.40

For women:

0.50 / 0.60

Now add those weights to the data.

toy <- toy |>
  mutate(
    weight = case_when(
      gender == "Man" ~ 1.25,
      gender == "Woman" ~ 0.83
    )
  )

Look at the result:

toy

6. Compare the raw and weighted estimates

First calculate the ordinary sample mean:

mean(
  toy$support
)

Now calculate the weighted mean:

weighted.mean(
  toy$support,
  toy$weight
)

The two results differ because the weighted estimate gives relatively more influence to the underrepresented group and relatively less influence to the overrepresented group.

A weight does not change what a respondent answered.

It changes how much that respondent contributes to the final estimate.

7. What happens in a real poll?

Real survey weights are usually more complicated than this example.

A professional poll may adjust for several characteristics at the same time, such as:

  • age;
  • gender;
  • race and ethnicity;
  • education;
  • geography;
  • language; and
  • features of the sampling or recruitment design.

Methods such as post-stratification, raking, or calibration can combine information from several variables.

We do not need to program those procedures ourselves in this lab.

The important idea is the same:

Respondents who are underrepresented relative to the target population generally receive more influence, while respondents who are overrepresented generally receive less.

14. Why would weights differ?

Possible reasons include:

  • some groups were oversampled;
  • some groups were underrepresented among respondents;
  • the sample does not perfectly match known population characteristics;
  • the survey design intentionally gave some people different chances of entering the sample.

We do not need to reconstruct every step of the weighting algorithm to use the supplied poll weights appropriately.

Part VII: What weighting can and cannot do

19. What weighting can help with

Weighting can help adjust for known differences between the sample and the population.

For example, weights may account for differences in:

  • age;
  • gender;
  • race or ethnicity;
  • education;
  • region; or
  • features of the sampling design.

20. What weighting cannot guarantee

Weighting does not automatically fix:

  • a badly worded question;
  • respondents who misunderstood the question;
  • people who were completely excluded from the recruitment process;
  • bogus or low-quality interviews;
  • all forms of nonresponse bias; or
  • an important difference that was never measured or used in weighting.

A weighted estimate is not automatically an unbiased estimate.

Weighting is an adjustment tool, not a guarantee of perfect representation.

21. A note about precision

When weights differ substantially, some respondents contribute much more than others.

That means a weighted sample can contain less statistical information than a simple sample of the same raw size.

This is the idea behind effective sample size and design effects.

We will discuss those concepts in lecture.

For this lab, you do not need to calculate them by hand.

What to remember

The practical workflow is:

# Raw estimate
mean(outcome, na.rm = TRUE)

# Weighted estimate
weighted.mean(
  outcome,
  weight,
  na.rm = TRUE
)

The important questions are:

Which population am I describing?

Which weight belongs to that population?

How much does weighting change the result?

What problems can weighting not solve?
